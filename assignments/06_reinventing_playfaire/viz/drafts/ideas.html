Wikipedia Arbitration · SARC 5400 Final Project · Experimental Views

Mapping Conflict

Arbitration is Wikipedia's highest court — but it's opaque by design. These five views are attempts to demystify it: to give shape to what conflict looks like from the inside, who bears the cost, how the process actually unfolds, and where the most damaging cases cluster in a 20-year history. Each uses the same 101 real arbitration cases, read five different ways.

Idea 01 — Historical Overview

The Record

Every case as a tremor in Wikipedia's 20-year history of conflict

Time runs left to right across two decades. Each horizontal line is one arbitration case — its length shows how long it ran, its thickness how many editors were involved, and its color what the dispute was about. Red ticks mark cases where more than 30% of participants left. Reading across, you can see conflict doesn't happen randomly: it arrives in waves, often triggered by real-world events, and certain types of dispute dominate certain eras.

Idea 02 — Conflict Topology

The Fingerprints

Each type of conflict has a shape — these radar profiles reveal it

No two arbitrations are alike, but cases about the same thing tend to share a profile. Each spider chart shows one topic's cases across six dimensions: how long they ran, how many editors were involved, the departure rate, the sanction rate, the density of activity, and how much of the work happened in the evidence phase. Individual cases are ghosted; the bold path is the topic average. Outlier shapes are cases that broke the mold for their type.

Idea 03 — Human Cost

The Exodus

Of all the editors who enter an arbitration, who actually makes it out?

Each bar represents every editor who entered an arbitration case in a given topic area. The green segment shows those who stayed through to the end. The red segment shows those who departed during or after. The orange shows those who were sanctioned. The gray remainder is ambiguous — their outcome isn't clearly recorded. The pattern reveals which types of conflict are most costly to the people inside them, and where the process is most likely to drive participants away.

Idea 04 — Process Architecture

The Journey

Where did the editorial work actually happen in each case?

Arbitration moves through stages — from the opening statement to evidence gathering, workshop, proposed decision, and outcome. But each case allocates its effort differently. Here every case is a horizontal strip, its width proportional to duration, divided into colored segments by phase weight. The left sidebar marks the topic. Reading top to bottom (oldest to newest), you can see how the architecture of conflict has changed — and how some cases are almost entirely evidence, while others burn through the workshop and proposed-decision phases.

Idea 05 — Risk Landscape

The Tension Map

Plotting every case by its human cost — where do the crises cluster?

This scatter map plots each case by two measures of human cost: how many editors departed (x axis) and how heavily participants were sanctioned relative to total editors (y axis). Circle size encodes duration. The dashed lines mark the median in each dimension, dividing the space into four zones — Clean, Punitive, Attrition, and Crisis. The cases that fall furthest into the Crisis quadrant are the ones arbitration failed to contain.
